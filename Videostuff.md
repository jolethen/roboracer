Hello everyone! Today, we are excited to walk you through the hardware architecture and electrical design of our robot. Building a high-performance robot isn't just about writing code; it requires a rock-solid power and wiring system to ensure everything runs smoothly without crashing or burning out. Let’s start with the heart of our power system: the 3S LiPo Battery. This battery outputs a nominal voltage of 11.1 volts. That high voltage is fantastic because it gives our DC motors the raw muscle and speed they need to sprint across the track. However, there’s a major catch: our brain, the ESP8266 NodeMCU microcontroller, is a delicate logic device that runs strictly on 3.3 volts. If we sent 11.1 volts directly into the ESP, it would instantly fry. To solve this, we integrated an LM2596 Buck Converter. A buck converter is a step-down DC-to-DC switching regulator. We carefully adjusted its output dial and used a multimeter to lock it at a steady, reliable 5 volts. This 5V line is fed directly into the Vin (or 5V) pin of our ESP8266. Why the Vin pin instead of the 3.3V pin? Because the Vin pin routes power through the ESP's onboard voltage regulator, which safely steps that 5 volts down to the precise 3.3 volts the chip needs while protecting it from minor voltage fluctuations.

Taking over from there, once the ESP8266 is safely powered and stable, it acts as the central brain of our entire setup. For our drive system, we are using two DC motors, each managed by its own dedicated motor driver. Both motor drivers receive high-power feed lines running directly from the main 11.1-volt battery. This means the heavy electrical current required to spin the motors under load bypasses the microcontroller entirely, preventing any system bottlenecks or component overheating. To achieve full mobility and steering control, our ESP8266 utilizes four independent GPIO control pins—two dedicated pins wired into Motor Driver 1, and two dedicated pins wired into Motor Driver 2. By sending pulse-width modulation (PWM) and digital signals through these lines, the ESP can independently control the speed and rotational direction of each motor. This allows our robot to execute sharp turns, accelerate rapidly, and maneuver precisely on the track without any mechanical steering lag.

That brings us to the final, and arguably most important, part of our build: putting all the physical wiring together cleanly and securely. When you're building a robot that moves fast, loose connections are your worst enemy. Vibrations on the track can easily shake a poorly twisted wire loose, causing random reboots or total power loss mid-run. To keep everything reliable without overcomplicating things, we focused on making sure every wire is securely joined, neatly routed, and properly insulated. We made sure all power lines are thick enough to handle the heavy current flowing from the battery to the motor drivers, while keeping our signal lines neat and tidy so they don't get tangled up with moving mechanical parts. Before putting the whole chassis together, we double-checked every connection with a multimeter—especially verifying that steady 5-volt output coming out of the buck converter so we don't accidentally fry our ESP8266. We also tested each motor driver individually using our ESP's GPIO control pins to confirm that both left and right wheels respond instantly and spin in the correct directions. With a stable 11.1-volt power supply, a safe 5-volt logic step-down, independent dual-driver motor control, and solid, dependable wiring, our robot’s hardware is fully optimized and ready to hit the track.
